Measuring playstyle in professional VALORANT

Do pro players have distinguishable playstyles — and is playstyle anything more than “how good they are” or “which agent they play”?

775 player-seasons · 400 players · 1,532 matches · 2023–2026 franchised VCT, from public vlr.gg records · snapshot 2026-09-19

Finding 1

One robust style dimension, and a second that is marginal

Seven per-map behaviours — opening duels, assists, spike plants, headshot rate, credits spent, clutch situations and an attack-minus-defence skew — reduce to two dimensions under principal component analysis. Each counts a situation the player put themselves in, never whether it went well; deaths was dropped for being an outcome rather than a situation.

How many dimensions are real is settled by a permutation test, not by a variance threshold. Each column is shuffled independently, 500 times — a Monte Carlo null that breaks every link between behaviours while keeping each one’s own distribution. A dimension survives only if it captures more variation than 95% of those shuffles produce. This is Horn’s parallel analysis in its permutation form, used instead of the usual normal-random version because these features are skewed.

PC1 aggression3.1701.190+1.9800.993

Eigenvalue = how many of the seven behaviours’ worth of variation a dimension captures; 1.0 is what one behaviour contributes on its own. Margin = eigenvalue minus the chance cutoff; above zero, the dimension is real. Reproduces = agreement between the dimension fitted on two random halves of the players, where 1.0 is identical and 0 is unrelated.

Aggression beats chance by a factor of nearly three. Isolation beats it by 0.060 — and among the 501 player-seasons that spend at least 70% of their maps in one role, it fails. Sample size is not the explanation: subsampling the full data down to the same n keeps it most of the time.

Isolation is real — it reproduces at 0.966 and holds up on an unseen year — but it is second-order, and this report treats it as such.

Finding 2

Riot’s role taxonomy recovers one of these dimensions, not four

A player’s role here is the one they played most: every map is assigned a role by the agent picked, and the most common one wins. Knowing it predicts 61% of where they sit on aggression — which sounds like the measure is just restating the role label. It is not.

predictor of a player’s aggression scoreR²n

R² = the share of the differences between players that the predictor accounts for; 1.0 would mean the label tells you the score exactly, 0 that it tells you nothing. The last two rows are computed on their own smaller group, so they are not directly comparable to the first two. Setting the duelists aside removes the largest source of variation — the spread among the remaining 570 is a third of the spread across all 775 — so 0.164 is 16% of what is left, which is about 5% of the original.

The full four-way taxonomy adds 0.052 over a single yes/no duelist flag. Almost everything the taxonomy knows about aggression is one bit: duelist, or not.

Set the duelists aside and knowing which of the remaining three roles a player has accounts for 16% of the spread still there — about 5% of the original. Restricting further to the 416 player-seasons that never picked a duelist agent once, which removes any label question rather than adjusting for it, gives 17%, with 106 of them (25%) sitting above the median aggression score for the whole population.

Where each role sits: 97% of duelists are above the overall median aggression, against 61% of sentinels, 30% of controllers and 21% of initiators. One category separates cleanly; the other three overlap.

The taxonomy answers one question: are you a duelist. It says almost nothing about how the other three roles are played. See it in the explorer →

Finding 3

It holds on a year the model never saw

Out-of-sample validation. Everything — the quality adjustment, the standardisation, the PCA rotation — was refitted on 2023–2025 alone. The 2026 season was then projected through that fixed machinery, so it contributed nothing to any fitted quantity and could not leak into its own test.

The test: for the 130 players present in both 2025 and 2026, does a player’s position in one year predict their position in the next?

correlation, 2025 position vs 2026 positionaggressionisolation

Both rows cover the same 130 players. A correlation of 1.0 would mean every player landed in exactly the same relative position in both years.

Hiding the year cost 0.007 on aggression and 0.032 on isolation. The measure is not fitting noise specific to the seasons it was built on.

Case study

Testing a popular claim

Fans have argued for years that aspas is a stat-padder — that his numbers come from avoiding the round’s first fight while teammates take it.

The general result first, because it reframes the question. Across the 149 duelist-seasons with a solid role label, a duelist who takes more opening duels dies more often (r = +0.38) and deals more damage (r = +0.29). Deaths are K/D’s denominator and damage drives its numerator, so the two pull it in opposite directions. Net effect on K/D: r = +0.08, which is nothing.

aspas — 2023–2026, 4 seasons, 3 teams, duelist on every map aspaspercentile
opening duels taken, per map6.0415th
deaths, per map14.033rd
kills ÷ deaths, per season1.2897th
damage, per round151.979th

The premise holds. He takes the first fight less often than 85% of duelists, and below the duelist median in all four seasons. The stated mechanism does not, because entering less does not raise K/D — the result above says the two are unrelated.

Percentiles are against the 149 duelist-seasons whose label is solid: at least 70% of maps on duelist agents. Against all 205 duelist-labelled seasons he is 31st percentile, but the 56 that fail the label test are part-time duelists averaging 5.45 opening duels a map against 6.95 for full-time ones — a comparison the claim does not intend.

It is a team pattern, not one player. Paper Rex is the least entry-heavy roster in the data: its duelists average 5.54 opening duels a map across 6 seasons, against 6.95 for all duelists, and a full point below the next-lowest team (LEVIATÁN, 6.56). jinggg averages 5.12, the lowest of any duelist measured in more than one season.

What this cannot show. Baiting is a claim about intent, and about teammates dying while you hold back. This measures neither. A disciplined duelist playing late timings and a genuine baiter look identical here.

Negative result

Discrete archetypes do not exist here

The original goal was to find player types. Clustering always returns clusters, so the question is not what the groups are but whether any exist. Two tests, both against the same control: a synthetic null drawn from a multivariate normal with the real data’s mean and covariance — same shape and spread, no groups in it.

Do the boundaries land in the same place twice? Yes. Splitting the players in half, running k-means on each half separately and comparing the two labellings by adjusted Rand index gives 0.73 at three groups, against 0.42 for the structureless cloud. (ARI of 1.0 means the halves group every pair of players identically, 0 means chance. A Gaussian mixture returns the same verdict.)

Is there empty space between the groups? No. The silhouette score is 0.365, against 0.315 for that same cloud — where 1.0 would mean groups with clear gaps between them and 0 means none. The groups touch everywhere.

Both hold at once: a continuum with denser patches gives repeatable cutting lines without the patches being separate populations. At three groups, 18% of player-seasons sit close enough to a boundary that their label is arbitrary. Positions on the two axes are the output; the groups are a description laid over them.

Method

Five decisions that changed the answer

Sixteen decisions are recorded, each with its diagnostic, its measured cost and what would overturn it. These five mattered most.

Measure style, not skillEvery behaviour is residualized on K/D — regressed on it, and only the residual kept — rather than on combat score. Combat score accumulates with how much a player fights, so it is inflated by aggression itself — the very thing being measured. A ratio cancels fight volume out. The average correlation between a raw behaviour and the yardstick is .389 for combat score against .205 for K/D, so K/D leaves less skill in the style measure to begin with.
Confounder, not mediatorEvery quality measure is partly caused by playstyle, so adjusting for all of them would delete real style along with skill. K/D is the one least likely to sit on that causal path. The choice is to under-correct and state which way the remaining bias runs.
Correct for rare eventsA player faces a median of 13 clutch situations a season, so a year-to-year correlation is dragged down by measurement noise — attenuation. Reliability is estimated by split-half correlation over 200 random splits, scaled up with the Spearman–Brown formula, and the observed stability divided by it. Reversed two of nine feature decisions.
Player, not rosterStability was re-measured on the 104 players who changed organisation between seasons. Clutch rate fell from .35 among players who stayed to .01 among those who moved — a team assignment wearing a player trait’s costume.
Do not control for agentPlayers keep their most-played agent 34% of the time when they stay on a roster and 30% when they leave, so agent choice travels with the player, not the team. Role is held out as a validation label and never enters the model, so cross-role structure is shown rather than engineered.

Limitations

What this does not show

  • The second dimension is marginal. Isolation reproduces across halves at 0.966 and holds on the unseen year, but beats chance by only 0.060 and fails among single-role players. Treat aggression as the finding and isolation as a secondary reading.
  • The name of the second dimension is an interpretation, not a result. “Lurking” fits five of seven predictions made in advance, including a lower trade rate (r = −0.28), which is direct evidence of playing away from teammates. It fails the two that would separate lurking from a general low-utility, high-aim style. Settling it needs player positions and within-round timing; this dataset has neither.
  • One row averages several jobs. A player uses a median of 5 agents a season, and 23% of player-seasons spend under 60% of their maps in a single role. For those the season average blends jobs done separately — their year-to-year stability on aggression is 0.47, against 0.87 for players who spend 80–95% of their maps in one role. The grain cannot be made finer: a median player-agent-season is only 4 maps, too few to measure the weaker behaviours at all.
  • The agent label is weak; the role label is not. A player’s most-played agent covers a median of 43% of their maps, their most-played role 81%. Role-level claims are safe here, agent-level ones are not.
  • 2026 is incomplete. The season is still running and has no Champions event, so the held-out year is missing the tier where the strongest teams meet, about 7% of a normal year’s maps. Stated, not corrected for.
  • Associational only. Nothing here claims that a style causes a result.
