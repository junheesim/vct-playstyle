Measuring playstyle in professional VALORANT

Do pro players have distinguishable playstyles — and is playstyle anything more than “how good they are” or “which agent they play”?

775 player-seasons · 400 players · 1,532 matches · 2023–2026 franchised VCT, from public vlr.gg records · snapshot 2026-09-19

Before we start

How VALORANT works

  • Two teams of five. One side attacks, the other defends, and they swap at halftime. First to 13 rounds wins the map; a match is two or three maps.
  • The attackers carry a bomb — the spike — and try to plant it on one of the map’s sites. The defenders try to stop them, or to defuse it once it is down.
  • Each player picks an agent, a character with its own abilities, bought alongside weapons with credits earned during the game.
  • Riot sorts the agents into four roles. Duelists take fights and open up sites. Initiators clear space with information and stuns. Controllers block sightlines with smoke. Sentinels hold ground and watch the flank.
  • The round’s first fight is the opening duel — the most dangerous moment to be in, and the one duelists are expected to take. Being left alive alone against several enemies is a clutch.
  • One row of data here is one player’s season: every map they played that year, a median of 37 of them.

In short

What this found

Professional players do have playstyles that can be measured, that persist from one season to the next, and that are not simply a restatement of how good they are.

Almost all of the difference between players lies on a single dimension: how much a player seeks out fights themselves, rather than enabling the fights their teammates take.

Riot’s own four-way classification of agents captures one part of that almost perfectly, and the rest of it hardly at all. It tells you decisively whether a player is a duelist. Below that line it separates the players who anchor a site from those who do not, and says nothing useful about the difference between the remaining two roles.

And there are no player types. The differences between players are continuous. Any set of groups drawn on this data is a description laid over a cloud, not a discovery of separate populations.

What went in

What was measured, and why

A scoreboard mostly measures how good a player is. Feed those columns into a model and the leading result is a quality ranking wearing a costume. So the six behaviors below were chosen on two principles.

Measure a player’s behavioral tendencies, not whether their outcomes were successful. How often a player takes the round’s first fight is a tendency. Whether they win it is an outcome.

And cover the whole round. A round has a shape: you buy, you take the first fight, the middle plays out, and it ends. A set that measured only the opening would produce an axis that was really about early-round aggression and nothing else, while claiming to be about aggression. So there is a behavior for each phase.

Measurable in one season is how far two random halves of a player’s season agree about them, where 1.0 would mean the halves give the same answer. Correlation with quality is how closely the behavior tracks that player’s kills divided by deaths — near zero means the behavior describes style, and large either way means it is mostly restating how good the player is. None of the six comes close enough to be quality wearing a different name.

Two things then decide whether a tendency is usable: whether one season contains enough of it to measure, and whether it is anything more than quality under another name. Four further candidates were screened the same way and dropped — deaths, KAST, spike defuses and an attack-minus-defense skew. Method says what happened to each.

The endgame is the phase this set measures worst, and the table shows it. Clutch situations is by far the weakest measurement here: a player faces only about a dozen a season, so much of what is recorded is luck rather than the player, and a player who changes organization keeps almost none of their clutch rate — so part of what it captures is the seat rather than the person. It stays because it is the only behavior covering how rounds end. Dropping it would leave the axis blind to the endgame entirely, and a missing phase is a systematic gap where noise is only random error. That is a deliberate trade, and it is the weakest link in this set.

Finding 1

One robust style dimension, and a second that is marginal

The six behaviors above reduce to two dimensions under principal component analysis.

How many dimensions are real is settled by a permutation test, not by a variance threshold. Each column is shuffled independently, 500 times — a Monte Carlo null that breaks every link between behaviors while keeping each one’s own distribution. A dimension survives only if it captures more variation than 95% of those shuffles produce. This is Horn’s parallel analysis in its permutation form, used instead of the usual normal-random version because these features are skewed.

The eigenvalue is how much of the variation across the six behaviors that dimension accounts for, and the share of variation is the same figure as a percentage of the whole. The Monte Carlo null is what the same calculation returns on shuffled data: 500 shuffles, and the column gives the figure only 5% of them exceed. The margin is how far above it the real dimension sits. Reproduces is how closely the dimension comes out the same when the players are split in half and it is fitted on each half separately. Two dimensions clear the null and the third does not, so the analysis keeps two — and isolation clears it by 0.071, the thin margin every later section returns to.

Aggression (PC1) clears the null comfortably. Isolation (PC2) barely does — and there is one group of players where it does not clear it at all.

Nearly a third of player-seasons are split between two roles, so their season average describes neither job cleanly. Restrict to the 501 seasons where a player spent at least 70% of their maps in one role — the cleanest measurement in the data — and run the same test again. Only one dimension survives. Isolation’s margin does not merely shrink: it falls from +0.071 to −0.035, below the null.

That could simply be the smaller sample — drop a third of the rows and a marginal result would weaken anyway. So the same test was run on 100 random samples of 501 seasons, drawn with no role restriction at all. Isolation survived in 99 of them, and not one fell as far below the cutoff as the role specialists did. The restriction is what removes it, not the sample size.

Isolation is still real. It reproduces when the players are split into two halves and the analysis is run separately on each, and it holds on a season the model never saw. But it is second-order, and this report treats it as such throughout.

Finding 2

Four named roles, three distinguishable levels

A player’s role here is the one they played most: every map is assigned a role by the agent picked, and the most common role across the season wins.

Knowing a player’s role predicts a large share of where they sit on aggression — which sounds like the measure is just restating the label. It is not, and the way it fails is specific.

R² is the share of the differences in aggression between players that the predictor accounts for. The last two rows are computed on their own smaller group, so they are not directly comparable to the first two: setting the duelists aside removes the largest source of variation, so 0.175 is around 18% of what is left rather than of the original. The last row keeps only seasons in which the player never picked a duelist agent at all, which removes any question of the label being wrong.

Almost all of what the taxonomy knows is a single yes/no question. A binary classification — duelist, or not (R² = .523) — does nearly as well as the full four-way label (R² = .586). Set the duelists aside, and knowing which of the remaining three roles a player has accounts for only 18% of the aggression spread among them.

That could be blamed on the labels. A player labeled an initiator may still have spent part of the season on duelist agents, and that blurring could be what makes the taxonomy look weak. So the same test was run on the 416 player-seasons that never picked a duelist agent at all — a group with nothing to mislabel. The answer barely moves (R² = .185).

Above 2.0 the two barely overlap at all; around 0.2 is a difference you would not notice watching them play.

The gaps are not evenly spaced. At one end of the taxonomy, duelists and initiators barely overlap at all (2.80): of the 416 players who never picked a duelist agent, exactly one reaches the median duelist’s aggression, and only 0.5% of duelists fall below the median non-duelist. At the other end, controllers and initiators are the same role played twice (0.32) — a difference you would not notice watching them. Sentinels sit genuinely in between: about a standard deviation above controllers and initiators, and nearly two below duelists.

So four named roles resolve into three levels, and one gap does almost all the work. The taxonomy tells you decisively whether a player is a duelist. Finding 3

Playstyle persists from one season to the next

A style that lasted one season would be a circumstance, not a trait. So: for the 130 players who appear in both 2025 and 2026, does knowing where a player sat on the aggression axis in one season tell you where they sat in the next?

The answer is a correlation, where 1 would mean every player landed in exactly the same relative position both years and 0 would mean one year tells you nothing at all about the next. Aggression comes out at 0.731, isolation at 0.693.

Is that a lot? On its own the number means nothing, so here is the same correlation, across the same two seasons and the same players, for measures a reader already has a feel for.

Each figure is the correlation between a player’s value in 2025 and their value in 2026, over the 130 players in both seasons. Read down: a player’s headshot rate is the most repeatable thing about them, and their team’s win rate the least.

A player’s style is more persistent than their results. Aggression holds from one season to the next more strongly than that player’s own kill-death ratio, and far more strongly than whether their team wins. That is what you would expect of a disposition rather than a performance, and it is the plainest evidence in this report that style is not quality under another name. One caveat: aggression is built from six measurements averaged together, and averaging cancels noise, so some of its advantage is mechanical — though headshot rate is a single measurement and still beats it.

What this does and does not establish. It measures whether a player keeps their position, which is a fact about people. It says nothing about whether the axes themselves would be found again from data they were not built on — that is a different question, and it is tested in Robustness.

Case study

Testing a popular claim

This is the question the project started from. aspas is one of the most decorated duelists in the game, and fans have argued for years that he is a stat-padder: that his numbers come from avoiding the round’s first fight and letting teammates take it instead. The stronger version of the accusation is baiting — deliberately holding back so that teammates die in fights he could have joined, which protects his own record at their expense.

That is three separate claims, and they can be tested separately: that he avoids the first fight, that avoiding it is what produces his numbers, and that he plays apart from his team.

Start with the second claim, because it can be tested directly. aspas takes 6.04 opening duels a map. Twenty-two duelist-seasons in this data take even fewer — he sits at the low end of a populated range, not off the end of it. If entering less were what produced a high kill-death ratio, those twenty-two would have high ratios.

They do not. Those twenty-two average 1.05, against 1.06 for duelists as a whole. aspas is at 1.28. The players doing exactly what he is accused of doing have entirely ordinary ratios.

The same holds across the whole range. An opening duel resolves, by definition, into either a first kill or a first death for the player who took it, so taking more of them adds to both sides of the ratio at once. Across the 149 duelist-seasons with a solid role label, duelists who enter more do die more often (r = +0.38) and deal more damage (r = +0.29) — but how often a duelist enters is essentially unrelated to their kill-death ratio (r = +0.08). Here r is a correlation across those seasons, where 0 means no relationship and 1 would mean a perfect one.

Entering less does not come with a better kill-death ratio. That is an association across players, not a claim about what would happen to one player who changed their game. But it is exactly the association the accusation depends on, and it is not there — aspas’s ratio sits 0.23 above what a duelist entering as little as he does is predicted to have, the 97th percentile of that gap.

Against all 205 duelist-labeled seasons he is 31st percentile on opening duels, but the 56 that fail the label test are part-time duelists averaging 5.45 opening duels a map against 6.95 for full-time ones, which is not the comparison the claim intends.

The first claim holds, and more strongly than the raw count suggests. On the aggression axis he sits at the 8th percentile among duelists — lower than his opening-duel count alone puts him, because the axis also counts how little he spends and which side he is aggressive on. He is below the duelist median in all four seasons.

The third claim does not hold. If he were playing away from his team, the isolation axis is where it would show: that dimension is what separates players who shoot a lot and contribute little utility from players embedded in their team’s rounds. He sits at the 60th percentile — the duelist average.

Two numbers do look like baiting at first. He is last alive more often than 79% of duelists, and assists less often than all but 13% of them. But you cannot be the last player alive if you died early, and aspas dies less often than all but 3% of duelists. Being alive at the end goes with not dying (r = −0.28 between clutch situations and deaths). Ask instead what clutch rate a duelist who dies as rarely as he does normally has, and he falls to the 63rd percentile — unremarkable.

So the premise holds and the explanation does not. He really does take the first fight far less than other duelists. But among duelists there is no trade-off between entering and kills-per-death for that to work through, and neither measure that would show him playing apart from his team shows it.

It is a team pattern, not one player. Paper Rex has the least entry-heavy duelists in the data — 5.54 opening duels a map across 6 seasons, against 6.95 for duelists as a whole — and they are also the least isolated, at −0.89 against −0.30. Their duelists take fewer first fights while playing closer to their team, not further from it. Taking fewer opening duels and playing alone are different things, and these two axes can tell them apart.

What this cannot show. Baiting is a claim about intent, and about teammates dying in fights the player chose not to join. Nothing here measures either. A disciplined duelist who picks their moments and a genuine baiter would look the same in this data.

Negative result

Discrete archetypes do not exist here

The project set out to find player types. But clustering always returns clusters: ask any method for three groups and it gives you three, from any data at all, including a cloud with nothing in it. So the question is never what the groups are. It is whether the groups mean anything.

The measurement is separation. Take one player. Measure how far they sit, on average, from the other players in their own group — call that A. Then measure how far they sit, on average, from the players in the nearest other group — call that B.

If B is much larger than A, that player sits well inside their own group with a gap before the next one. If the two are about the same, they are on the fence between two groups. That player’s score is how much bigger B is than A, as a share of the larger of the two, and the separation figure is that averaged over every player. 1 would mean every player is far from every group but their own. 0 would mean everybody is as close to the neighboring group as to their own — the groups touching everywhere, with nothing between them.

On its own a separation figure means little, so the quickest way to read one is beside data where the answer is already known.

The data looks like the panel on the left, not the one on the right — and the numbers say so more precisely than the pictures can. Separation, the figure under each panel, averages 0.320 ± 0.010 across 200 structureless clouds. The real players separate at 0.369, about five times that spread above the floor — so there is genuinely a little more clumping here than in a cloud with nothing in it. But three types as distinct as duelists are from initiators separate at 0.527 ± 0.008. The real data sits 3 times closer to no grouping than to three types: 0.048 above the floor, 0.159 below the ceiling. Being measurably above nothing is not the same as being anywhere near something.

The same thing shows up player by player: at three groups, 139 player-seasons — 18% of them — sit close enough to a boundary that which group they land in is arbitrary.

The boundaries are repeatable, and that is not a contradiction. Split the players in half, find the groups in each half separately, and the two halves agree about who belongs with whom far more than they would on a structureless cloud (0.738 against 0.377 ± 0.128). A loaf can be cut in the same place twice without the slices being separate objects. That is what a continuum with a consistent shape does, and it is why the three regions can describe the space even though they are not populations.

Positions on the two axes are the finding. Any groups drawn over them are a description, not a discovery.

Robustness

Does any of it rest on a judgment call?

Three choices in this analysis were argued rather than derived: what to adjust for, how many maps a season needs before it counts, and whether to count behaviors per map or per round. Each is run end to end, and what it changes is reported.

1. Adjusting for quality. Every behavior is measured against what a player of equal quality would do, and quality is measured by kills divided by deaths. Two other choices were available, and they disagree.

Role explains is the share of the differences in aggression that the role label accounts for; quality left in is how strongly a player’s finished aggression score still tracks their combat score. Unadjusted, “aggression” is substantially “is good at the game”, which is the confusion this project exists to remove — and adjusting for combat score removes more of it, along with real style.

Adjusting for combat score looks better on both columns, and it is the wrong choice. Combat score is accumulated from combat events, so a player who takes more fights scores higher at the same level of skill — subtract it and you subtract part of the aggression being measured along with the skill. Kills divided by deaths does not accumulate that way: an opening duel adds one to the kills or one to the deaths, so taking more of them moves both sides of the ratio at once. The choice is to under-correct rather than over-correct, and the cost is the 0.586 rather than 0.354 in the first column: some of what looks like role is really quality that was left in.

2. The season-length floor, the one cutoff with no principled value. A player needs 15 maps in a season to be included. Swept from 5 to 30 — a six-fold change in strictness that takes the sample from 880 player-seasons down to 555:

Aggression, 2025 to 2026 is Finding 3’s persistence figure — how strongly a player’s aggression in one season predicts their aggression in the next — recomputed at each floor, on the players who qualify at it: 148 at a floor of 5, 130 at 15, 82 at 30. 15 is the floor used throughout this report.

No figure moves by more than 0.043 across the whole sweep, and two dimensions are kept at every floor. Where a cutoff could not be derived it was swept rather than defended, which turns an arbitrary choice into a reported non-issue.

3. Per map or per round. A map is first to 13 rounds, so it runs anywhere from 13 to 48 rounds, and four of the six behaviors are counts averaged per map rather than rates per round. A player whose team wins quickly has fewer chances to do anything. Rebuilt from the top with every count converted to a rate, players land in the same positions at r = 0.998. The reason it does not matter is that the opportunity count does not vary between players: a season averages 37 maps, and across players the average map length runs from 20.4 rounds to 22.0.

4. Would the axes be found again from data they were not built on? Everything so far was fitted on all four seasons at once. Fit the whole thing on 2026 alone instead — 197 player-seasons, never mixed with the rest — and compare the axes that come out.

Aggression comes back as the same axis, built from the same behaviors in almost exactly the same proportions (0.999, where 1.0 would be identical). Isolation does not come back at all: on 2026 by itself, only one dimension clears the Monte Carlo null from Finding 1. Part of that is the smaller sample — the same test on 100 random samples of 197 seasons drawn from the other years keeps two dimensions 70 times, so failing at this size is not unusual. It is suggestive rather than decisive, and it is the fourth independent sign pointing the same way about the second dimension.

And one restriction that is not a judgment call but a limit of the data. Nearly a quarter of player-seasons are spent switching between roles, so their season average describes a blend of two jobs. Restricted to the 501 seasons that spend at least 70% of their maps in a single role, aggression is unchanged — but only one dimension survives. That is the isolation result from Finding 1, appearing again from a different direction.

Method

Five decisions that changed the answer

Each of these could have gone the other way, and each one moved a number in this report.

Screen the behaviors, do not threshold themEvery candidate on the scoreboard was put through the same three questions: is it a tendency or an outcome, can one season measure it, and is it anything more than quality renamed. No cutoff was set for any of them. There is no accepted value for “reliable enough” or “too close to quality” to borrow, so any number chosen would have been invented and would then have decided the feature set quietly. Each verdict is written out with its reason instead. Deaths is the case that shows it working: it passes both measurable tests and was dropped anyway, because a death is a fight lost.
Measure style, not qualityEvery behavior is measured against what a player of equal quality would do — the behavior is predicted from the player’s kills-per-death, and only the part that prediction misses is kept. Combat score was the obvious alternative and is the wrong one: it accumulates from combat events, so a player who takes more fights scores higher at the same level of skill, and subtracting it would subtract part of the aggression being measured. Averaged across the six behaviors, a raw behavior correlates .386 with combat score and only .212 with kills-per-death — so the measure we adjust for starts out less entangled with the behaviors it is meant to strip quality out of.
Correct for rare eventsA player faces a median of 12 clutch situations a season. Measure anything from twelve events and the measurement is half luck — so if a player’s clutch rate looks different this year from last, that may be the player changing or may be the noise. The two are separated by splitting each season in half at random and asking whether the halves agree about the player. Whatever they disagree about is noise, and the year-to-year figure is scaled up by exactly that much. Two features had been judged unusable on their raw numbers and survived this correction.
Player, not rosterA behavior that looks like a personal trait may be a job the coach assigned. The test is what survives a transfer: measure each behavior again on only the 104 players who changed organization between seasons. Most hold up — headshot rate and opening duels barely move. Clutch rate collapses: a player’s clutch rate correlates .35 from one season to the next among players who stayed on the same roster, and .01 among those who moved. A player who changes roster keeps essentially none of it. It is kept as a behavior anyway, because being last alive is one of the few things in this data that is not determined by a player’s role — but on this evidence it describes the seat more than the person, and that is stated rather than buried.
Do not control for agentPlayers keep their most-played agent 35% of the time when they stay on a roster and 35% when they leave — no difference at all — so agent choice travels with the player rather than the team, and subtracting it would subtract part of the style. Role is held out as a check on the finished measurement and never enters the model. Controlling for it would have made the headline claim true by construction: remove role, then show the answer is not role.

Limitations

Where the finding is weaker than it looks. These are limits of this data and this sample. A larger or cleaner source would ease them.

  • The second dimension is marginal. Isolation reproduces across halves at 0.970 and holds on the unseen year, but clears its Monte Carlo null by only 0.071 and fails among single-role players. Treat aggression as the finding and isolation as a secondary reading.
  • One row averages several jobs. A player uses a median of 5 agents a season, and 23% of player-seasons spend under 60% of their maps in a single role. For those the season average blends jobs done separately — their year-to-year stability on aggression is 0.47, against 0.87 for players who spend 80–95% of their maps in one role. The grain cannot be made finer: a median player-agent-season is only 4 maps, too few to measure the weaker behaviors at all.
  • Assists depend on teammates finishing the fight. An assist is credited only if the enemy dies, so unlike the other five behaviors it is partly conditional on the round going the team’s way. It is also the behavior most determined by role. It stays because how much a player builds their game around teammates is exactly the tendency this report is trying to measure, but it is the least clean of the six on the principle the set was chosen by.
  • 775 rows are not 775 independent observations. They come from 400 players, up to four seasons each, and every R² and correlation above treats them as independent. The point estimates are unaffected; the precision around them is overstated, by roughly the ratio of rows to players. Where it was cheap to avoid, it was: the split-half replication in Finding 1 splits by player, never by row, because a row-wise split puts the same player in both halves. The role R² figures are not corrected for it.
  • 2026 is incomplete. The season is still running and has no Champions event, so the held-out year is missing the tier where the strongest teams meet, about 7% of a normal year’s maps. Stated, not corrected for.
  • The agent label is weak; the role label is not. A player’s most-played agent covers a median of 43% of their maps, their most-played role 81%. Role-level claims are safe here, agent-level ones are not.

What this cannot answer at all. These do not improve with more of the same data. Answering them needs a different kind of record entirely.

  • The name of the second dimension is an interpretation, not a result. “Lurking” — playing away from teammates, hunting flanks and late picks — fits five of seven predictions written down in advance. The clearest is that players high on this dimension are less often traded, meaning a teammate rarely kills the enemy who just killed them (r = −0.26). Being traded requires a teammate nearby, so that is direct evidence of playing alone. It fails the two predictions that would separate lurking from a general low-utility, high-aim style. Settling it needs player positions and within-round timing; this dataset has neither.
  • Attacking and defending are not separated. An earlier version carried the attack-minus-defense skew as a seventh behavior, and it was dropped: only four of the six behaviors can be split by side at all, so keeping it gave one of them a dimension the others could not have. The cost is that a duelist who opens sites on attack and a player who takes early duels defending now land in the same place, with identical totals and opposite styles.
  • Associational only. Nothing here claims that a style causes a result.
